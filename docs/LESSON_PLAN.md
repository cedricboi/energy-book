# LESSON_PLAN.md: The Energy Ledger

Put this file in `docs/`. It is the content plan for the book. `CLAUDE.md` holds the rules. This file holds what each lesson teaches.

**Status: approved after Phase 1 (18 Nov 2026), with the six changes from `docs/SYLLABUS_COVERAGE.md` applied.** The syllabus is the K223/K224/K225 G2 Science SEC syllabus in `docs/syllabus.pdf`. The book has 8 lessons and a chapter game. Anything marked **[verify]** is a fact that must be confirmed with a web search and a cited source (recorded in `src/lessons/sources.js`) before it goes in front of students. Hooks are suggestions. The teacher may swap any of them.

Each lesson follows the five stages: hook, investigated scenario, concept, test, check for understanding. Each opens with today's two questions and closes with an exit ticket that returns to them.

---

## Lesson 1. Energy stores

**Today's questions.** Where is energy kept? How can you tell which store it is in?

**Hook.** A phone is at 100% in the morning and dead by night. The energy has not vanished. Where did it go? (The hook returns at the end of Lesson 2.)

**Scene.** A shelf of six glass jars on the page, one per store, each with its pattern and icon. Below them sit everyday objects: a stretched rubber band on a ruler catapult, a book held above a desk, a rolling toy car, a battery, a cup of hot kopi and a small sun in the corner.

**Explore (no predict).** Students tap each object. Its energy rises out of the object as glowing colour and settles in the matching jar. After the first three, students drag the remaining objects to the jar they think matches, then tap to check.

**Terms named, in order.** Energy store, then each store name as its object is met: kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, chemical potential, internal, nuclear. These six match the syllabus and the teacher's notes.

**Use and check.** One question at a time, on new cases: a durian hanging on a tree, a drawn bow, a bowl of rice, a hot iron, the uranium fuel in a power station. MCQ with parallel options, then one short written answer.

**Notes page.** A blank ledger with six rows. Students drag each store name to its description.

## Lesson 2. Energy transfers

**Today's questions.** How does energy move from one store to another? What are the four ways it can move?

**Hook.** The Lesson 1 phone returns. It charges from a power bank, gets warm, lights up and plays a song. Each of those is a transfer.

**Scene.** The jars from Lesson 1 sit on the page, joined by four kinds of glowing paths. Each pathway has its own line style: a pushing arrow for energy transferred mechanically, a current line for energy transferred electrically, a wavy heat shimmer for energy transferred by heating, and ripples for energy transferred by propagation of waves.

**Explore (no predict).** Students run four short scenes: a ruler catapult firing an eraser, an electric kettle boiling water, a torch switched on (light, an electromagnetic wave), and a phone speaker playing a note (sound, a mechanical wave). For each one they watch energy leave one jar and arrive in others along a pathway, then pause the scene and tap the pathway that carried it.

**Terms named.** Energy transfer, then the four ways: transferred mechanically (by a force acting over a distance), transferred electrically (by an electric current), transferred by heating (due to a temperature difference), transferred by propagation of waves (both electromagnetic and mechanical).

**Use and check.** Students build the transfer chain for new devices (a hand-crank radio, a hair dryer, a solar garden light) by choosing a store, then a pathway, then a store.

**Close the loop.** Back to the phone. Students write where its 100% went.

**Notes page.** Students label a chain diagram for a torch.

## Lesson 3. Kinetic energy

**Today's questions.** What does the energy in a kinetic store depend on? How do we calculate it?

**Hook.** Braking distance at higher speeds. A car at a higher speed needs far more than proportionally more distance to stop. Use a real road-safety figure for braking distance at two speeds. **[verify, prefer Singapore Traffic Police or LTA data]**

**Scene.** A toy car on a level track, rolling into a block of soft clay. The dent in the clay grows with the car's kinetic energy (energy transferred to the clay's internal store). A ruler beside the track and the clay is drawn to scale.

**Explore (no predict).** Sliders with − and + buttons for mass (0.2 kg to 0.5 kg, in steps of 0.1 kg) and speed (1.0 m/s to 3.0 m/s, in steps of 0.5 m/s). These ranges still allow doubling (0.2 kg to 0.4 kg, 1.0 m/s to 2.0 m/s, 1.5 m/s to 3.0 m/s). They keep the smallest energy (0.10 J) at about 4% of the largest (2.25 J), so it still shows in the jar. If that sliver is hard to read, the jar uses a ×10 scale step with a visible note (`CLAUDE.md` section 5, rule 5). The kinetic jar fills live. Students double the mass and see the energy double. They double the speed and see the energy become four times as large. A small table records each run the student makes.

**Terms named.** Kinetic energy, then the equation Ek = ½mv² with units.

**Use and check.** Calculations on new cases (a cyclist, a thrown netball, a bus). One question deliberately catches students who do not square v, and its feedback names that mistake.

**Close the loop.** Back to braking distance. Students explain, using only kinetic energy, why doubling speed has such a large effect: at twice the speed the car has four times the energy in its kinetic store, and all of it must be removed to stop the car. Do not use "work done" here, because it is not named until Lesson 7. Lesson 7 returns to braking distance and completes the explanation.

**Notes page.** A worked-example layout where students fill in the steps.

## Lesson 4. Gravitational potential energy

**Today's questions.** What does the energy in a gravitational potential store depend on? How do we calculate it?

**Hook.** A durian falling from a tall tree hits harder than one falling from a short tree, which is why people are warned not to stand under durian trees at harvest time. **[verify, use a cited source on typical durian tree heights and fruit mass]**

**Scene.** A bookshelf with shelves at 0.5 m, 1.0 m, 1.5 m and 2.0 m, drawn to scale beside a metre rule. A box can be lifted onto any shelf.

**Explore (no predict).** Students drag the box to each shelf and change its mass. The gravitational potential jar fills. Students find that doubling the height or the mass doubles the energy.

**Terms named.** Gravitational potential energy, gravitational field strength (g = 10 N/kg), then Ep = mgh.

**Use and check.** Calculations on new cases (a student climbing stairs, a lift, a raised drawbridge). One question asks for h when Ep and m are given.

**Close the loop.** Back to the durians. Students calculate and compare Ep for two given heights.

**Notes page.** Students fill a worked example and a units box.

## Lesson 5. Energy on a ramp (conservation of energy)

**This is the sample lesson built first in Phase 5.**

**Today's questions.** Where does the energy go as a skateboarder rolls down a ramp? How fast is the skateboarder at the bottom?

**Hook.** A playground slide or a skatepark half-pipe. Use a real photo of a local skatepark. **[teacher to supply photo]**

**Scene.** A smooth half-pipe on the page, drawn to scale with height markings. A skateboarder (a simple ink figure) starts at rest at a chosen height. A ledger bar under the ramp shows the gravitational potential, kinetic and internal stores stacked, with a line marking the total.

**Predict (everyday, so allowed).** "A skateboarder starts at rest at the top of one side. With no friction, how high will they rise on the other side?" Options are lower, the same height or higher, and students must give a reason. Then students run it.

**Explore.** Sliders for start height (0.5 m to 3.0 m) and mass. A friction switch. With friction off, the energy swaps between gravitational potential and kinetic stores and the total stays level. With friction on, the internal store of the board and ramp grows with each pass, and the rider never returns to the same height. A "pause at the bottom" button lets students read the speed.

**Terms named.** Principle of conservation of energy, in the notes' wording: energy cannot be created or destroyed, energy can be transferred from one store to another, and the total energy of an isolated system is constant. Then the phrase "transferred to the internal store of the surroundings" for what friction does.

**Use and check.** Using mgh = ½mv² to find the speed at the bottom, and showing it does not depend on mass. Apply it to a new case (a roller coaster, a ball rolling down a slope).

**Close the loop.** Back to the playground slide. Students explain why a real slide never sends you back up to the top.

**Notes page.** Students complete the statement of the principle of conservation of energy (5(c) asks students to state it), then a ledger at the top, middle and bottom of the ramp.

## Lesson 6. Why a bouncing ball stops

**Today's questions.** If energy is conserved, why does a bouncing ball stop? Where does the energy end up?

**Hook.** Everyone has dropped a ball and watched it bounce lower each time. A ball that bounced back to the same height every time would never stop. Why does that never happen?

**Scene.** A ball dropped onto the desk beside a metre rule, with a ledger bar. During the squash, an elastic potential store appears. Each bounce sends a small amount of energy to the internal store (of the ball and the floor) and to sound waves, which ripple away from the contact point.

**Predict (everyday, so allowed).** "You drop a tennis ball from 1.0 m. How high will it bounce?" Students give a value and a reason, then drop it.

**Explore.** Choose the ball: tennis ball, basketball, clay ball or "superball". Change the drop height. Step through one bounce frame by frame to see each transfer. The total line stays level the whole time. The share in the internal store and waves grows.

**Terms named.** Elastic potential store (met again). The phrase "transferred to the internal store of the surroundings" is used again. Energy is never destroyed.

**Use and check.** New cases: a pendulum slowly stopping, a car braking to rest, a trampoline jumper.

**Energy in equals energy out (from the old Lesson 9).** One use page shows a device ledger, for example a lamp that receives 60 J electrically each second and gives out 6 J by light waves. Students find where the other 54 J went and name the store. All in joules. No efficiency and no percentage.

**Close the loop.** Students answer the hook in their own words, using "internal store of the surroundings" correctly.

**Notes page.** A bounce ledger for one bounce, to be completed.

## Lesson 7. Work done

**Today's questions.** When is work done on an object? How do we calculate it?

**Hook.** A student holds a heavy school bag perfectly still for ten minutes and is exhausted. In physics, they have done no work on the bag. How can that be?

**Scene.** A crate on a floor, a push force arrow drawn to scale, and a distance marker. A wall nearby.

**Explore (no predict).** Sliders for force (0 N to 200 N, in steps of 10 N) and distance (0 m to 5 m, in steps of 0.5 m). Work done ranges from 0 J to 1000 J, so the jar uses a ×10 scale step with a visible note when the value is below 100 J (`CLAUDE.md` section 5, rule 5). Zero force or zero distance always shows an empty jar. The energy transferred mechanically flows from the person's chemical store into the crate (kinetic store, then the internal store through friction). Students push the wall with a large force, see the distance stay at zero, and see no energy transferred to the wall.

**Terms named.** Work done, then the syllabus word equation (work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force), then W = F × d as in the teacher's notes, then the joule as a newton metre. Work done equals the energy transferred mechanically.

**Use and check.** Calculations on new cases, including one where the force is applied but the object does not move.

**Close the loop.** Back to the school bag. Students explain why no work is done on the bag while it is held still.

**Link back to Lesson 3.** One use question returns to braking distance. The brakes do work on the car to empty its kinetic store. With the same braking force, four times the energy needs four times the distance. Students complete this explanation using W = F × d.

**Notes page.** A fill-in worked example with units.

## Lesson 8. Power

**Today's questions.** What is power? How do we compare how quickly two things transfer energy?

**Hook.** Two students carry the same bag up the same staircase. One runs and one walks. They do the same work. What is different?

**Scene.** A staircase drawn to scale, two identical figures carrying identical loads, and a stopwatch for each.

**Explore (no predict).** Students set each person's time and watch the gravitational potential jar of each load fill at a different rate. A second scene compares a 1000 W and a 2000 W kettle heating the same water.

**Terms named.** Power, the watt, then the syllabus word equation (power = energy transferred ÷ time taken) and P = E/t, written as a fraction. Kettle power is given in watts. P = VI is not used, because it belongs to Topic 12.

**Use and check.** Calculations on new cases (a lift motor, a phone charger, a sprinter). One question gives the time in minutes, to check unit conversion.

**Close the loop.** Back to the staircase. Students calculate both powers and state who has the greater power.

**Notes page.** Students match each equation to its units and complete a worked example.

## Chapter game. Ledger Run

A chain-reaction machine game where the physics is the mechanic.

- Each level is a marble machine drawn in ink: ramps, springs, a lift, a fan and a target cup. Students set the heights, spring compressions and motor times.
- Each part states how many joules it transfers to the internal store of the surroundings (for example "this ramp loses 0.02 J"). No efficiency or percentages, because they are not in the syllabus. The game computes every transfer with the same physics module as the lessons. **There is no fake physics.**
- The marble reaches the target only if the student's energy accounting is right. Too little energy and it stalls. Too much and it overshoots.
- **Points.** Points for reaching the target, a bonus for using the least input energy, and a streak bonus for levels cleared first time. No speed bonus.
- **Levels.** 8 levels, from one ramp to a five-part machine. A win screen after level 8.
- Students can only earn points by doing the energy accounting correctly. There is no way to guess through a level by trial and error alone, because the inputs are limited.

## Scope notes

- Specific heat capacity, energy resources (renewable and non-renewable) and Sankey diagrams are **left out**. Phase 1 confirmed they are not in the G2 syllabus. Efficiency and the electrostatic store are also out, for the same reason.
- The teacher may want these lessons regrouped into fewer, longer sessions. The engine supports any grouping, because lessons are data.
